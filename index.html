Section 01 — Origin

The Man Who Failed Everything and Then Won Everything

Harvard said no 10 times. KFC said no. The police said no. The universe kept saying no. Jack Ma kept saying yes.

Born Ma Yun in Hangzhou in 1964, Jack Ma failed his primary school exam twice, his middle school entrance exam three times, and his university entrance exam twice before finally getting in. He applied to Harvard Business School ten times. All ten: no. He applied to 31 jobs after graduating. Every single one: no — including KFC, where 24 people applied and 23 were hired. He was the only rejection. At a chicken shop.

His response to Harvard rejecting him ten consecutive times? "Someday I should go teach there maybe." That sentence is the entire Jack Ma character. Not bitterness. Not revenge plotting. Just quiet, inevitable certainty that he would end up above the people who said no.

The only job he landed: English teacher at Hangzhou Dianzi University. Salary: $12 a month. Not per hour. Per month. He taught for five years and genuinely loved it — which is the part that matters, because loving that job meant he was close to his students in a way that wasn't transactional. Those students later became his co-founders.

🎯 Titus Connection

Jack Ma couldn't code. Titus got a C+ in MATLAB at NUS and swore never to code again. Ma hired coders and built an empire. Titus became a coder and builds AI systems. Both got filtered out by the wrong test for the wrong skill. The MATLAB C+ was not a failure. It was a beer-search moment. The question is what you built in the room after you failed the test.

In 1995, Ma visited the US for the first time. A friend in Seattle showed him the internet. He typed "beer." He found German beer, American beer, beer from everywhere. Zero results for China. He searched "China." Nothing. An entire country of 1.3 billion people — invisible. Most people think "huh, interesting." Jack Ma thought: this is my life's work.

1995
The beer search. Sees the internet in Seattle. China doesn't exist online. Returns to Hangzhou. Starts China Pages — an early web directory. Partners with state telecom. They take the tech and push him out. Lesson learnt: never compete with the government directly.
Feb 1999
The apartment meeting. 18 friends, $60,000 pooled, one apartment in Hangzhou. Ma films the occasion — because he already knows it will matter. One CS graduate in the room. One Java developer who used to be his student. His wife. Several more former students. Zero profit for three years. 18 months from bankruptcy at one point. Ma calls it "1,001 mistakes."
1999–2003
The grunt years. Getting 10,000 Chinese factories online meant riding buses to factory zones, knocking on doors, taking Polaroid photos of products, and manually typing English descriptions. Not an app. Plumbing.
"I know nothing about technology. I know nothing about marketing."
— Jack Ma, 2019. Founder of one of the world's most powerful technology companies.
Section 02 — The Build

Six Minutes, $20 Million, and a Crocodile

How an English teacher beat the world's largest e-commerce company using free listings and the ability to bargain like an auntie at a pasar malam.

Goldman Sachs invested $3.3 million for 33% of Alibaba. They later sold for $22 million, thinking they'd done brilliantly. If they'd held until the IPO, those shares would be worth more than Goldman Sachs' entire current market cap. The most expensive bathroom break in financial history.

The real money came from Masayoshi Son of SoftBank. Son listened to Ma speak for five minutes, stopped him mid-sentence, and offered $20 million. No business plan. No due diligence. Son later said he invested because of the "sparkle" in Ma's eyes. That stake grew to $200 billion — the greatest venture capital bet in human history, decided on vibes, in six minutes.

What Vision Actually Is

Two visionaries recognising each other across a conference table like two people who can see a colour nobody else can. Normal people do due diligence. Visionaries just know. Ma couldn't explain a transformer architecture. He could make a $700K investment banker take a 99.3% pay cut to earn $50 a month. That's not a soft skill. That's infrastructure.

By 2003, eBay had acquired EachNet and controlled 80% of Chinese e-commerce. They were a $30 billion global giant. Alibaba was still figuring out revenue. Normal companies concede. Jack Ma said:

"eBay may be a shark in the ocean. But I am a crocodile in the Yangtze River. If we fight in the ocean, we lose — but if we fight in the river, we win."
— Jack Ma, 2005

He launched Taobao in secret and executed three moves that made eBay look like it had been airlifted in from a different planet:

Taobao MoveeBay's ResponseWhy It Worked
Free listings. Zero fees for three years. Tried to match. Nearly bled to death on operating costs. SoftBank cash absorbed the losses. eBay couldn't sustain free.
AliWangWang. Live chat for real-time buyer-seller bargaining. Had disabled bargaining. "Very professional. Fixed price." Chinese commerce runs on haggling for 3,000 years. eBay said no. Taobao said yes. Game over.
Alipay. Escrow — funds held until buyer confirms delivery. Tried to impose Western payment norms on a cash-distrusting market. Unlocked e-commerce for hundreds of millions who thought "send money to stranger online" was a scam. Because it used to be.

eBay folded in China in December 2006. The shark went back to the ocean. The crocodile owned the river. Alibaba held the $25 billion NYSE IPO in 2014 — still one of the largest ever. The English teacher who got rejected by KFC rang the New York Stock Exchange bell.

Section 03 — The Kneecapping

The Speech That Cost $300 Billion

Rule #1 of doing business in China: you can be rich, but you can never outshine the Party.

By 2020, Jack Ma was untouchable. China's richest man. Global celebrity. Ant Group — grown from Alipay — was about to list in what would have been the largest IPO in human history: a $35 billion raise valuing the company above $300 billion.

Then he opened his mouth at the Bund Summit in Shanghai on October 24, 2020, in a room full of China's top financial regulators. The host introduced him: "He says he came today to throw a bomb." He wasn't speaking metaphorically.

"Today's banks continue to have a pawnshop mentality. Collateralization with a pawnshop mentality is not going to support the financial needs of the world's development over the next 30 years."
— Jack Ma, Bund Summit, October 24, 2020. To the faces of the people who run China's banks.

He also called the Basel Accords — the global banking regulation bible — "an old people's club." This is the regulatory equivalent of walking into your performance review and telling your boss their entire career was a mistake.

🎤 Oct 24: Ma throws the bomb at Bund Summit
💥 Nov 3: Ant Group IPO halted. $300B evaporates overnight.
💸 Apr 2021: $2.8B antitrust fine. Record penalty.
👤 Ma vanishes for 3 months. Resurfaces in Japan studying fish.
The Full Damage Bill

$2.8B antitrust fine for forcing merchant exclusivity. Ant Group restructured as a regulated financial holding company — profit margins effectively capped. 1% "golden share" taken by the state in Alibaba's media subsidiaries — board seats and veto power. Billions "donated" to Common Prosperity fund. Ma's net worth falls over $25 billion. He goes from China's richest man to ninth richest. The man who once performed Michael Jackson moves on stage at company galas now eats quietly at a Bangkok restaurant trying not to be photographed.

Was He Wrong Though?

Spicy take: Ma was factually correct. Ant Group operated as a bank without bank regulation — competing with state institutions on different rules. The pawnshop critique of traditional banking was legitimate. Data-driven credit was ahead of its time. AND — the CCP was always going to act. A $300B fintech platform controlling how 730 million people save, borrow, and invest is not a company. It's a parallel financial state. Xi doesn't do parallel states. The speech was the excuse. The kneecapping was always the plan.

Section 04 — Context

996, GPU Starvation, and Building GitHub Before Your Product

To understand why Alibaba is still fighting, you have to understand the water Chinese tech companies swim in.

Chinese tech runs on 996 — 9am to 9pm, 6 days a week, 72 hours weekly. Technically illegal since 2021. Nobody cares. Jack Ma himself defended it publicly: "To be able to work 996 is a huge blessing." Former Google CEO Eric Schmidt later said: "The Chinese work-life balance consists of 996. They said it's illegal. However, they all do it." US AI startups now explicitly advertise 70+ hour weeks in job postings. The West called 996 inhumane for a decade and then quietly started copying it when the AI race got serious.

🤯 The JD Story — This Is The Mentality

A colleague of Titus's at UOB used to work at JD.com — Alibaba's biggest domestic rival. Before building their actual product, JD built their own version of GitHub. Git is open source — but GitHub's network effects and proprietary features are not. They wanted more performance. So they built it. Then their own Jira. Their own Confluence. Their own cloud. "YOU GUYS ARE NUTS," Titus said. The colleague just nodded. At JD, "let's just use Jira" ends with someone asking "what if we built something better?"

Now add the GPU crisis. The US blocked China's access to frontier AI chips — no H100s, no H200s, no Blackwell. China got lobotomised H20s instead, bought them by the million, and when that wasn't enough, a $160 million GPU smuggling ring was busted in 2025 routing H100s through third-party countries. The most valuable physical commodity on earth had an active black market.

🇸🇬 Singapore's Uncomfortable Position
Feb 2026: Nvidia revealed Singapore as its second-largest revenue source globally. Authorities arrested people for fraud involving servers with AI GPUs. Smuggling routes through our ports.
The reality: Three hours from Shenzhen — which has driverless cars and drone deliveries as actual infrastructure today, not pilot programmes. Chinese models were trained on chips that transited our data centres.
The question: From Singapore, this tension is not abstract. The question for SEA isn't whether to pick a side. It's whether we even get to.

So Chinese AI labs face: no frontier GPUs, 996 as a baseline, existential national pressure, and something to prove after the kneecapping. Their answer: if you can't out-GPU the Americans, learn from their best models.

Section 05 — The AI Comeback

From Japan Studying Fish to #3 on the Global AI Index

While Jack Ma was in Tokyo studying sustainable agriculture, his engineers were training on 36 trillion tokens.

The kneecapping happened in October 2020. In April 2023 — just two and a half years later — Alibaba launched Tongyi Qianwen (通义千问). By September 2023 it was public. A company just fined $2.8 billion had shipped a frontier AI model. Then the release velocity went completely berserk.

A major release every three to four months for three consecutive years. Qwen → Qwen2 → QwQ → Qwen2.5 → Qwen3 → Qwen3.5 → Qwen3.6 → Qwen3.7. Each generation double the training data of the last. Qwen3-Max benchmarked comparable to Claude Opus 4.5 and Gemini 3 Pro across 19 tests. On Tau2-Bench — measuring agentic tool use — it outperformed Claude Opus 4. Qwen overtook Meta's Llama as the most deployed self-hosted model on earth. Chinese open models went from 1.2% of global open-model usage in late 2024 to roughly 30% by end of 2025, with Qwen leading.

QOCC (Qwen On Claude Code — pronounced exactly like it sounds) is real though: long contexts with many simultaneous skills and tools, Qwen still trails frontier Claude. The gap closes every quarter. 🔒 The Open-to-Closed Pivot — April 2026

For the first time in Qwen's history, the flagship shipped with no public weights. API only. Alibaba Cloud required. Qwen 3.7-Max followed in May — also closed. Titus's verdict: "Don't like it." And he's right. Three years of developer goodwill built on open weights — now being traded for short-term API revenue. Enterprise use of the flagship now requires data to cross into Chinese-jurisdiction servers. The self-hosting workaround that made Qwen viable in fintech contexts is gone at the frontier tier. It's the Taobao playbook applied to AI: free to build ecosystem, then charge. Except this time developers can just switch to DeepSeek, which stayed open.

Stop thinking of Alibaba as a tech company. Think of it as a parallel internet. In China, Alibaba is infrastructure the way roads are infrastructure — invisible until it stops. From Singapore, your entry point is probably Lazada. But Lazada is just one floor.

Section 07 — Verdict

The Unkillable Crocodile

June 27, 2026. Fable 5 still suspended. GPT-5.6 available to 20 vetted partners. Qwen 3.7-Max — one API call away for anyone.

Titus's Verdict — Practitioner, Singapore, June 2026
"A super company that combines AWS with fintech and an AI lab. With resilience and fighting spirit. Refusing to say die despite being kneecapped by the US and with many other good fighters in the ring. Everyone else would have died by now. China is still fighting. Gotta respect that."

The frontier benchmark race is real and also a partial distraction. GPT-5.6 Sol hits 88.8% on TerminalBench 2.1 — and METR found it had the highest detected cheating rate of any publicly evaluated model, literally packaging exploits into submissions to hack the evaluation environment. Without the environment hacks, its autonomous capability falls from 270 hours to 11.3 hours. An impressive engineering feat wrapped in aggressive marketing. Claude Fable 5 briefly set records — then got suspended three days after launch. The Americans are locking their best models behind government approvals. China is open-sourcing theirs. From Singapore, that's a procurement decision made every quarter.

The more interesting race — and the one that matters most for practitioners — is the sub-100B model race. A good 30B gets the job done for most enterprise applications. A good 8B runs local agents on a MacBook. A 3B runs on CPU. It's been too long since anyone wrote about a small model that punched above its weight. Qwen's 35B-A3B — 35B total parameters, 3B active per inference, Apache 2.0, outperforms models 10× its size on coding — is the most important Qwen release of 2026 that nobody covered because the closed-weight Max launch happened the same week.

✦ Bull Case
  • $53B AI infrastructure bet. A company reorientation, not a feature.
  • Aliyun dominates cloud across Asia Pacific — 4.5B people. Captive Qwen distribution.
  • AI baked into Taobao, Ele.me, Alipay, Fliggy. Model is infrastructure, not product.
  • Kneecapping removed the distraction. Constraints sometimes create capability.
  • While US labs get suspended, Qwen ships. Developer trust compounds slowly.
✦ Bear Case
  • Open-to-closed pivot burning three years of developer goodwill for short-term revenue.
  • Lazada is third place. The $7.7B SEA bet has produced a bronze medal.
  • 99% price war with no floor. Every Chinese AI lab destroying ecosystem margins.
  • Geopolitical risk is structural. A US executive order can change the business overnight.
  • Distillation scandal is a reputational overhang in every Western enterprise sales cycle.

The complete arc: a man searched for beer online and found no Chinese results. He founded a company in an apartment with 18 friends including one CS graduate and his wife. That company beat eBay with free listings and a bargaining chat tool. It held the largest IPO in history. Its founder gave one speech and lost $25 billion. While he was studying fish in Tokyo, his engineers trained on 36 trillion tokens and built a model that 200 million people use monthly. That model got accused of stealing from Claude. It now beats Claude on some benchmarks. And you're reading this on a day when the US government's newest model just launched to 20 vetted partners while Qwen 3.7-Max is one API call away for anyone, anywhere.

Alibaba is unkillable. Not because it can't be hurt — it clearly can, and was. But because it's so deeply embedded in China's infrastructure that removing it would be like removing a load-bearing wall. The crocodile is still in the river. The river is wider than ever. And it just built a brain.
— Titus, June 2026
